# Arterial Blood Gases and Acid-Base Analysis

An **arterial blood gas (ABG)** measures the pH, the partial pressures of oxygen and carbon dioxide, and bicarbonate in arterial blood. It tells you how well the patient is **oxygenating, ventilating, and maintaining acid-base balance.** It is one of the most important tests in critical care.

## Normal Values

* **pH:** **7.35 to 7.45**
* **PaCO2** (partial pressure of carbon dioxide): **35 to 45 mmHg**
* **PaO2** (partial pressure of oxygen): about **80 to 100 mmHg** (on room air)
* **HCO3** (bicarbonate): **22 to 28 mmol/L**
* **SaO2** (arterial oxygen saturation): **95 to 100 percent**
* **Base excess:** about **-2 to +2**
* **Lactate** (often reported with the gas): below about **2 mmol/L**

## Obtaining an Arterial Sample

### Site
* **Radial artery** (most common, easy to reach and compress, with collateral ulnar supply)
* **Brachial or femoral arteries** if needed
* **Arterial line** in critical patients, for frequent sampling

### Technique
* Use a **heparinized syringe** (or a pre-heparinized gas kit)
* **Palpate the pulse,** clean the skin, anesthetize with local anesthetic, insert at about 30 to 45 degrees, and allow the **arterial pressure to fill the syringe**
* **Remove air bubbles** and **seal the syringe**
* **Apply firm pressure for at least 5 minutes** (longer in anticoagulated patients)
* **Analyze immediately** (within 15 minutes at room temperature, or on ice within 30 minutes). Cells keep consuming oxygen and producing CO2.
* Record the **FiO2** (or oxygen flow), the patient's temperature, and the time.

### Complications
Pain, hematoma, arterial spasm, thrombosis, infection, and rarely nerve injury and limb ischemia. **Avoid** sampling through infected skin, with **no pulse,** or when there is **a lack of collateral circulation.**

### Venous Blood Gas (VBG)
Easier to obtain. Compared with arterial blood:
* **pH is about 0.03 lower**
* **PCO2 is about 4 to 5 mmHg higher**
* **Bicarbonate is similar**

A VBG is acceptable for estimating **pH, bicarbonate, and lactate.** It does **not** reliably assess **oxygenation** (use pulse oximetry or ABG).

## A Systematic Approach to Acid-Base Disorders

### Step 1: Look at the pH
* **Below 7.35:** **acidemia**
* **Above 7.45:** **alkalemia**
* **Normal:** may still have a mixed disorder

### Step 2: Identify the Primary Process
* **Acidemia with high PaCO2:** **respiratory acidosis**
* **Acidemia with low HCO3:** **metabolic acidosis**
* **Alkalemia with low PaCO2:** **respiratory alkalosis**
* **Alkalemia with high HCO3:** **metabolic alkalosis**

### Step 3: Check Whether Compensation Is Appropriate
The body compensates, but never overcorrects. If the compensation is not as expected, there is a **second, mixed disorder.**

#### Metabolic Acidosis
**Winter formula:** expected **PaCO2 = (1.5 × HCO3) + 8, plus or minus 2.**
* Actual PaCO2 **higher** than expected: additional **respiratory acidosis**
* Actual PaCO2 **lower** than expected: additional **respiratory alkalosis**

#### Metabolic Alkalosis
PaCO2 rises by about **0.7 mmHg for every 1 mmol/L rise in HCO3.**

#### Respiratory Acidosis
* **Acute:** HCO3 rises by about **1** for every **10 mmHg** rise in PaCO2
* **Chronic:** HCO3 rises by about **3.5 to 4** for every **10 mmHg** rise in PaCO2

#### Respiratory Alkalosis
* **Acute:** HCO3 falls by about **2** for every **10 mmHg** fall in PaCO2
* **Chronic:** HCO3 falls by about **4 to 5** for every **10 mmHg** fall in PaCO2

### Step 4: Calculate the Anion Gap (in Metabolic Acidosis)
**Anion gap = Na minus (Cl plus HCO3).** Normal about 8 to 12. Correct for albumin.

### Step 5: Delta-Delta (Delta Ratio)
In a high anion gap acidosis, compare the **rise in the anion gap** with the **fall in bicarbonate:**
* **Delta gap = (anion gap − 12)**
* **Delta bicarbonate = (24 − HCO3)**
* **Ratio about 1 to 2:** a pure high-gap acidosis
* **Ratio below 1:** a **concurrent non-gap acidosis**
* **Ratio above 2:** a **concurrent metabolic alkalosis** (or pre-existing high bicarbonate)

### Step 6: Interpret in Clinical Context
Always link the numbers to the patient, the symptoms, and the cause.

## Metabolic Acidosis

### High Anion Gap (MUDPILES)
* **M**ethanol
* **U**remia
* **D**KA and other ketoacidosis
* **P**ropylene glycol
* **I**soniazid and iron
* **L**actic acidosis
* **E**thylene glycol
* **S**alicylates

### Normal Anion Gap (Hyperchloremic)
* **Diarrhea** (loss of bicarbonate)
* **Renal tubular acidosis**
* **Large-volume saline infusion**
* **Acetazolamide**
* **Adrenal insufficiency** and aldosterone problems
* Remember **"HARDASS":** hyperalimentation, acetazolamide, renal tubular acidosis, diarrhea, adrenal insufficiency, spironolactone, saline

### Clinical Features and Management
Hyperventilation (**Kussmaul respirations**), confusion, and hypotension. **Treat the cause.** Bicarbonate is used only in selected situations (severe acidemia with pH below about 7.1 to 7.2, or specific poisonings).

## Metabolic Alkalosis

* **Causes:** **vomiting and nasogastric suction** (loss of acid), **diuretics** (contraction alkalosis), **hypokalemia,** **hyperaldosteronism,** Cushing syndrome, and **milk-alkali syndrome**
* **Urine chloride** helps classify:
* **Low (below 20):** chloride-responsive (vomiting, diuretics). Treated with **saline** and potassium.
* **High:** chloride-resistant (hyperaldosteronism, Cushing, severe hypokalemia)
* Features: weakness, cramps, arrhythmias, and tetany

## Respiratory Acidosis (Hypoventilation)

* **Causes:** **COPD exacerbation, severe asthma,** **opioid and sedative overdose,** neuromuscular weakness (Guillain-Barré, myasthenia gravis, ALS), **obesity hypoventilation,** brainstem injury, pneumonia, and chest wall problems
* **Features:** confusion, drowsiness, headache, flapping tremor, and warm flushed skin
* **Management:** support ventilation (**non-invasive ventilation** or intubation), treat the cause, reverse opioids (naloxone). **Avoid excessive oxygen** in chronic CO2 retainers (target SpO2 88 to 92 percent).

## Respiratory Alkalosis (Hyperventilation)

* **Causes:** **anxiety and panic,** **pain,** **hypoxemia,** **pulmonary embolism,** pneumonia, **pregnancy,** fever and sepsis, **salicylate poisoning** (a mixed picture of respiratory alkalosis and high anion gap metabolic acidosis), liver failure, and central nervous system disease
* **Features:** tingling around the mouth and in the hands, light-headedness, and carpopedal spasm (low ionized calcium)

> **Clinical Pearl:** **Salicylate (aspirin) poisoning** classically causes a **mixed respiratory alkalosis and high anion gap metabolic acidosis.** Think of it in any patient with unexplained tachypnea, tinnitus, and confusion.

## Oxygenation

### Causes of Hypoxemia
* **Hypoventilation:** high PaCO2, **normal A-a gradient**
* **Low inspired oxygen:** altitude
* **Ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) mismatch:** pneumonia, COPD, asthma, pulmonary embolism. **Improves with supplemental oxygen.**
* **Shunt:** blood passes through the lung without being oxygenated (ARDS, large pneumonia, atelectasis, congenital heart disease). **Responds poorly to supplemental oxygen.**
* **Diffusion limitation:** pulmonary fibrosis, emphysema

### Alveolar-Arterial (A-a) Gradient
* **PAO2 = FiO2 × (barometric pressure − 47) − (PaCO2 / 0.8)**
* **A-a gradient = PAO2 − PaO2**
* **Normal** about **(age / 4) + 4** mmHg (so about 10 to 15 in young adults, higher in older adults)
* **Normal gradient with hypoxemia:** hypoventilation or low inspired oxygen
* **Raised gradient:** V/Q mismatch, shunt, or diffusion problems

### PaO2/FiO2 Ratio (P/F Ratio)
Used to grade **acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS):**
* **Mild:** 200 to 300
* **Moderate:** 100 to 200
* **Severe:** below 100

(With PEEP of at least 5 cmH2O, bilateral opacities on imaging, and a non-cardiac cause.)

### Oxygen Content and Delivery
Oxygen is carried almost entirely on **hemoglobin.** **Anemia lowers oxygen content** even if PaO2 and SaO2 are normal.

## Co-oximetry and Abnormal Hemoglobins

Standard pulse oximeters use two wavelengths and cannot detect abnormal hemoglobins. **Co-oximetry** (on a blood gas analyzer) measures them directly.

* **Carbon monoxide poisoning:** **carboxyhemoglobin** is raised. **Pulse oximetry reads falsely normal** because it cannot tell carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin. Suspect it with headache, confusion, and a cherry-red appearance (late and rare). **Treat with 100 percent oxygen** (hyperbaric oxygen in selected cases).
* **Methemoglobinemia:** (drugs like dapsone, benzocaine, nitrites). Blood appears **chocolate brown,** the patient looks **cyanotic** with a **pulse oximeter reading stuck around 85 percent** despite oxygen, and **PaO2 is normal.** **Treat with methylene blue** (avoid in G6PD deficiency).

## Interpreting Examples

### Example 1
pH 7.22, PaCO2 28, HCO3 11, Na 140, Cl 105.
* **Acidemia:** metabolic acidosis (low HCO3)
* **Anion gap:** 140 − (105 + 11) = **24** (high)
* **Winter formula:** expected PaCO2 = 1.5 × 11 + 8 = 24.5, plus or minus 2. Actual 28 is slightly **above** the expected range, suggesting incomplete compensation or an added respiratory acidosis from fatigue.
* **Interpretation:** a **high anion gap metabolic acidosis** (consider DKA, lactic acidosis, toxins).

### Example 2
pH 7.28, PaCO2 70, HCO3 32, with known COPD.
* **Acidemia, high PaCO2:** respiratory acidosis
* **HCO3** is raised more than expected for an acute change (**chronic compensation**)
* **Interpretation:** **chronic respiratory acidosis** (compensated by a high bicarbonate), with a likely **acute-on-chronic** component given the low pH (acute COPD exacerbation).

### Example 3
pH 7.52, PaCO2 28, HCO3 22.
* **Alkalemia with low PaCO2:** respiratory alkalosis
* **Interpretation:** acute hyperventilation (anxiety, pain, hypoxia, PE, early sepsis). Check the oxygenation.

> **Clinical Pearl:** Treat the **patient,** not the gas. A "normal" ABG can mask a patient who is tiring. A patient with severe asthma whose PaCO2 is **rising toward normal** may be about to fail.

> **Summary**
> The ABG shows pH, PaCO2, PaO2, and bicarbonate. Work through the same steps each time: identify acidemia or alkalemia, find the primary disorder, check compensation (Winter formula for metabolic acidosis), calculate the anion gap, and use delta-delta to find hidden disorders. High anion gap acidosis is MUDPILES, and normal gap acidosis is diarrhea or renal tubular acidosis. Use the A-a gradient to separate hypoventilation from V/Q mismatch or shunt. Pulse oximetry misses carbon monoxide and methemoglobin, so use co-oximetry.
