# Spinal Cord and Spine Disorders

Spinal cord disease is a leading cause of paralysis, and some forms are among the most time-critical emergencies in neurology. The key skills are recognizing cord compression and cauda equina syndrome early, and knowing the cord syndromes.

## Spinal Cord Syndromes

### Complete Cord Lesion
Loss of all motor, sensory, and autonomic function **below the level of injury,** with a **sensory level.**

### Anterior Cord Syndrome
* **Loss of motor function** (corticospinal tract) and **pain and temperature** (spinothalamic tract) below the lesion
* **Preserved** dorsal column functions (vibration, position sense)
* Cause: **anterior spinal artery infarction** (aortic surgery, dissection), flexion injuries
* Poor prognosis

### Posterior Cord Syndrome
Loss of **vibration and proprioception** with preserved strength and pain sense. Rare. Cause: **tabes dorsalis (syphilis), B12 deficiency,** and posterior spinal artery infarction.

### Central Cord Syndrome
* **Weakness greater in the arms than the legs,** with variable sensory loss and bladder dysfunction
* Typical cause: **hyperextension injury in an older patient with cervical spondylosis**
* Better prognosis, especially for legs

### Brown-Séquard Syndrome (Hemisection)
* **Ipsilateral** weakness and loss of **vibration and proprioception** below the lesion
* **Contralateral** loss of **pain and temperature** (starting a few levels below)
* Causes: penetrating trauma (stab wound), tumors, and demyelination

### Conus Medullaris Syndrome
Lesion at **L1-L2.** Early **bladder and bowel dysfunction,** saddle anesthesia, sexual dysfunction, **perianal sensory loss,** and relatively **symmetric** and less severe leg weakness. Mixed UMN and LMN signs.

### Cauda Equina Syndrome
See below.

### Syringomyelia
A fluid-filled cavity within the cord, often in the cervical region.
* **Cape-like (suspended) loss of pain and temperature** over the shoulders and arms, with **preserved touch and proprioception** (dissociated sensory loss)
* Hand weakness and atrophy, painless burns and injuries
* Associated with **Chiari I malformation**

## Acute Spinal Cord Compression

A neurologic emergency. **Permanent paralysis can occur within hours.**

### Causes
* **Metastatic cancer** (spine metastases from breast, lung, prostate, myeloma). The most common cause of nontraumatic compression.
* **Spinal epidural abscess**
* **Epidural hematoma** (anticoagulation, trauma, after a spinal procedure)
* **Disc herniation** and spondylosis
* **Trauma** and fracture
* **Primary tumors:** meningioma, schwannoma, astrocytoma

### Features
* **Back pain** (often severe, worse lying down, with percussion tenderness)
* **Progressive weakness** of the legs
* **Sensory level**
* **Urinary retention** or incontinence (a late sign)
* **UMN signs** below the level (after the initial spinal shock)

### Management
* **Urgent MRI of the whole spine** (multiple levels can be involved)
* **Malignant cord compression:** **dexamethasone 10 mg IV** (loading dose, then continued), **urgent radiotherapy** or **surgical decompression**, oncology and neurosurgery involvement
* **Spinal epidural abscess:** **vancomycin plus a third-generation cephalosporin (and sometimes metronidazole),** with surgical drainage if neurologic signs or large collection
* **Epidural hematoma:** reverse anticoagulation and urgent decompression

## Spinal Epidural Abscess

* **Classic triad:** **back pain, fever, and neurologic deficit** (often not all present early)
* **Risk factors:** IV drug use, diabetes, immunosuppression, recent spinal procedure, bacteremia, and indwelling catheters
* **Organism:** *Staphylococcus aureus* most commonly
* **Evolution:** back pain &rarr; radicular pain &rarr; weakness &rarr; paralysis
* **Diagnosis:** raised ESR and CRP, **urgent MRI with contrast,** blood cultures
* Avoid LP through an infected area.

> **Clinical Pearl:** Fever with severe back pain in an IV drug user is a spinal epidural abscess until proven otherwise. The window to prevent paralysis is short.

## Cauda Equina Syndrome

Compression of the lumbosacral nerve roots below the conus. A surgical emergency.

### Causes
**Massive central lumbar disc herniation** (most common), tumors, epidural abscess or hematoma, trauma, and spinal stenosis.

### Red Flag Features
* **Severe low back pain** with **bilateral sciatica** or leg weakness
* **Saddle (perineal) numbness or anesthesia**
* **Urinary retention,** with later **overflow incontinence**
* **Bowel incontinence** or loss of anal tone
* **Sexual dysfunction**
* **Reduced or absent ankle jerks**
* Leg weakness that is **LMN in type** (flaccid, reflexes reduced)

### Management
* **Urgent MRI of the lumbosacral spine.**
* **Emergency neurosurgical decompression,** ideally within 24 to 48 hours of onset of retention. Outcomes are much better if done **before** bladder involvement is complete.
* Bladder scan to assess post-void residual volume.

> **Clinical Pearl:** Ask every patient with low back pain: "Any numbness in the groin or inner thighs? Any trouble passing urine or controlling your bowels?" A bladder scan is a simple bedside test.

## Radiculopathy and Back Pain

### Acute Low Back Pain
Most acute low back pain is **mechanical** and self-limited, improving in 4 to 6 weeks.

#### Red Flags for Serious Pathology
* **Cancer** (history, unexplained weight loss, age over 50)
* **Infection** (fever, IV drug use, immunosuppression)
* **Fracture** (trauma, osteoporosis, steroid use, age over 70)
* **Cauda equina syndrome** or progressive neurologic deficit
* Pain at night or at rest, or not improving

#### Management Without Red Flags
Reassurance, **stay active,** NSAIDs, heat, brief use of muscle relaxants if needed, and physiotherapy. **No imaging** is needed in the first 4 to 6 weeks. Avoid bed rest and opioids.

### Lumbar Radiculopathy ("Sciatica")
Compression or inflammation of a nerve root, usually from a **disc herniation.**

* **Radiating leg pain** in the distribution of a nerve root, **worse with coughing, sneezing, or straining**
* **Straight leg raise** reproduces the pain (L5 and S1 roots). The **femoral stretch test** is for upper lumbar roots.
* **L4:** knee extension weakness, **patellar reflex reduced,** medial leg numbness
* **L5:** **foot dorsiflexion and great toe extension weakness** (foot drop), numbness on the dorsum of the foot
* **S1:** plantarflexion weakness, **Achilles reflex reduced,** lateral foot numbness
* **Imaging (MRI)** only if red flags, severe or progressive deficit, or persistent symptoms beyond 6 weeks
* **Treatment:** most improve within 6 to 12 weeks. NSAIDs, physical therapy, neuropathic agents (gabapentin), short courses of oral steroids (limited benefit), epidural steroid injections, and **surgery (discectomy) for progressive deficits, cauda equina, or persistent disabling pain.**

### Cervical Radiculopathy
Neck and arm pain, with weakness and numbness in a root distribution. **C7 (triceps, middle finger) and C6 are most common.** Managed conservatively in most cases.

## Spinal Stenosis

### Lumbar Spinal Stenosis
* Older adults with narrowing of the spinal canal from degeneration
* **Neurogenic claudication:** leg pain, heaviness, and weakness on **walking and standing,** relieved by **sitting or bending forward** (as when leaning on a shopping cart)
* Normal pulses (differentiating from vascular claudication)
* **Treatment:** exercise, physical therapy, epidural injections, and surgical decompression for severe or refractory disease

### Cervical Spondylotic Myelopathy
The most common cause of spinal cord dysfunction in older adults.
* **Gradual** hand clumsiness (trouble with buttons and handwriting), **gait unsteadiness,** neck stiffness, and **bladder dysfunction** in advanced stages
* **Signs:** UMN signs in the legs (hyperreflexia, spasticity, Babinski), **Hoffmann sign,** and a **wide-based, spastic gait**
* **MRI** shows cord compression.
* **Treatment:** surgical decompression, and **avoidance of falls** and neck hyperextension.

## Non-Compressive Myelopathies

* **Transverse myelitis** (Lesson 14)
* **Subacute combined degeneration (vitamin B12 deficiency):** degeneration of the **dorsal columns and corticospinal tracts.** Presents with **numbness, sensory ataxia, spasticity, and weakness,** often with anemia and macrocytosis. **Treat with B12 replacement (injection).** Nitrous oxide abuse can precipitate it.
* **Spinal cord infarction:** sudden onset of back pain and weakness (anterior spinal artery syndrome)
* **HIV, HTLV-1 (tropical spastic paraparesis), syphilis**
* **Radiation myelopathy**
* **Spinal dural arteriovenous fistula:** slowly progressive myelopathy in middle-aged men

## Traumatic Spinal Cord Injury

### Initial Management
* **Spinal immobilization** (cervical collar and backboard) in any trauma with potential spinal injury
* **ABCs,** with careful airway management that minimizes neck movement
* **Neurogenic shock:** **hypotension with bradycardia** from loss of sympathetic tone (injury above T6). Treat with fluids and vasopressors. Different from hemorrhagic shock (tachycardia).
* **Maintain mean arterial pressure at 85 to 90 mmHg** for the first several days to maintain cord perfusion
* **Early surgical decompression and stabilization** for unstable fractures and compression
* **High-dose methylprednisolone** is **not recommended** routinely (no clear benefit, increased complications)
* **Prevent complications:** DVT prophylaxis, pressure ulcers, pneumonia, urinary tract infections, and autonomic dysreflexia

### Spinal Shock
Temporary loss of all reflexes and tone below the lesion, from hours to weeks. **Flaccid paralysis and areflexia,** then spasticity and hyperreflexia develop.

### ASIA Impairment Scale
Standard grading (A to E):
* **A:** complete, no motor or sensory function in the lowest sacral segments
* **B:** sensory incomplete
* **C and D:** motor incomplete
* **E:** normal

### Autonomic Dysreflexia
An emergency in patients with spinal cord injury **above T6.** A noxious stimulus below the level (full bladder, constipation, pressure ulcer, ingrown toenail) triggers an uncontrolled sympathetic surge.
* **Severe hypertension, pounding headache, sweating and flushing above the injury,** and **bradycardia**
* **Management:** **sit the patient upright,** loosen tight clothing, and **find and remove the trigger** (check the bladder first, catheter, then bowel). If blood pressure remains high, give a fast-acting antihypertensive (such as nifedipine or nitrates). It can cause stroke or seizure.

## Spinal Tumors

* **Extradural (most common):** metastases, from breast, lung, and prostate cancer, myeloma, and lymphoma
* **Intradural-extramedullary:** **meningioma, schwannoma, neurofibroma**
* **Intramedullary:** **ependymoma and astrocytoma**
* Presentation is progressive back pain and myelopathy.
* **MRI with contrast** is the key test.

> **Summary**
> Cord syndromes localize lesions: central cord (arms worse than legs), anterior (motor and pain loss), Brown-Séquard (ipsilateral weakness and position loss with contralateral pain loss), and syringomyelia (cape-like pain loss). Cord compression and epidural abscess need urgent MRI, with steroids, radiotherapy, antibiotics, or surgery. Cauda equina syndrome (saddle numbness, urinary retention, bilateral leg weakness) is a surgical emergency. Most back pain is benign and does not need imaging. In trauma, immobilize, treat neurogenic shock, maintain MAP 85 to 90, and watch for autonomic dysreflexia above T6.
