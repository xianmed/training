# Retinal Detachment and Macular Disease

This lesson covers the conditions that separate the retina from its support (retinal tears and detachment) and the diseases that damage the macula, the center of sharp vision.

## The Vitreous and Posterior Vitreous Detachment

The vitreous is a clear gel that fills the back of the eye and is attached to the retina. With age it liquefies and shrinks, and eventually separates from the retina. This is called **posterior vitreous detachment (PVD).**

### Presentation
* **Flashes of light (photopsias)**, usually in the peripheral vision, from the vitreous tugging on the retina
* **New floaters:** spots, cobwebs, a ring, or a hair (a Weiss ring)
* Common after age 50, in myopia, and after cataract surgery

### Why It Matters
Most PVDs are harmless. However, in about **10 to 15 percent**, the vitreous pulls hard enough to cause a **retinal tear**, which can progress to a detachment. Anyone with new flashes or floaters needs a **prompt dilated examination of the entire peripheral retina**.

> **Clinical Pearl:** Any patient with a sudden shower of floaters, new flashes, or a shadow or curtain in their vision must be examined urgently. Do not reassure them without a dilated exam.

## Retinal Tears

Treated with **laser retinopexy** or **cryotherapy**, which create scars that seal the retina in place. Done early, treatment prevents detachment in most cases.

## Retinal Detachment (RD)

Separation of the neurosensory retina from the underlying retinal pigment epithelium. Without treatment it leads to permanent blindness.

### Rhegmatogenous Retinal Detachment
The most common type. A retinal break allows fluid from the vitreous to pass under the retina and lift it.

#### Risk Factors
* **High myopia**
* **Cataract surgery** (especially with complications)
* **Trauma**
* **Lattice degeneration** (areas of thin peripheral retina)
* **Previous retinal detachment** or family history
* Age

### Tractional Retinal Detachment
Scar tissue pulls the retina off. Classically seen in **proliferative diabetic retinopathy**, sickle cell disease, and after trauma.

### Exudative (Serous) Retinal Detachment
Fluid accumulates under the retina without any break. Caused by tumors, severe inflammation, central serous disease, and hypertensive emergencies. The fluid shifts with position.

### Symptoms
* **Photopsias** (flashes)
* **A shower of new floaters**
* **A curtain, shadow, or veil** progressing across the visual field
* **Central vision loss** when the macula detaches (called "macula-off")
* **Painless**

### Signs
* **RAPD** if extensive
* Reduced visual acuity if the macula is involved
* Often a **lower IOP** in the affected eye
* Dilated exam: a gray, elevated, wrinkled retina that moves like a sail in the vitreous, with the retinal break often visible
* **Tobacco dust (Shafer sign):** pigment cells in the vitreous
* Ultrasound if the view is blocked

### Urgency: Macula-On Versus Macula-Off
* **Macula-on:** central vision still preserved. A true emergency, because surgery can save sharp central vision. Often operated within 24 hours.
* **Macula-off:** central vision lost. Still needs urgent surgery, ideally within days, since outcomes worsen the longer the macula is detached.

### Treatment
* **Pneumatic retinopexy:** a gas bubble is injected and the retina is sealed with freezing or laser. Suitable for selected superior breaks.
* **Scleral buckle:** a silicone band is sewn around the eye to indent the wall toward the break.
* **Vitrectomy:** the vitreous is removed and the retina flattened, then held with a gas bubble or silicone oil.
* **Postoperative positioning** may be required to keep the bubble against the break.

> **Warning:** Patients with intraocular gas bubbles must **not fly or go to high altitude** and must not receive **nitrous oxide anesthesia,** because the gas expands and can cause blinding pressure spikes. They usually wear a wristband to alert clinicians.

## Vitreous Hemorrhage

Blood in the vitreous causes a sudden onset of floaters, a red haze, or marked loss of vision.

* **Causes:** proliferative diabetic retinopathy (most common), retinal tears, vein occlusions, trauma, and neovascularization.
* **Evaluation:** the retina may be hidden, so use **B-scan ultrasound** to rule out retinal tear or detachment.
* **Management:** treat the underlying cause, sit upright to let blood settle, and observe. Vitrectomy if it does not clear or if there is a tear or detachment.

## Age-Related Macular Degeneration (AMD)

The leading cause of irreversible central vision loss in older adults in developed countries.

### Risk Factors
* **Age** (strongest)
* **Smoking** (strongest modifiable)
* **Family history and genetics**
* White ancestry
* Hypertension, obesity, and poor diet

### Dry (Non-neovascular) AMD
About 85 to 90 percent of cases.

* **Drusen:** yellow deposits under the retina, the hallmark
* Gradual, mild loss of central vision
* **Geographic atrophy:** areas of retinal cell death in advanced disease
* **Treatment:** the **AREDS2 supplement formula** (vitamins C and E, zinc, copper, lutein, and zeaxanthin) reduces progression in intermediate and advanced disease. **Stop smoking**, eat leafy greens and fish, and monitor with an Amsler grid. Newer complement inhibitors for geographic atrophy exist.

### Wet (Neovascular) AMD
About 10 to 15 percent of cases but responsible for most severe vision loss.

* **Choroidal neovascularization (CNV):** abnormal vessels grow under the retina and leak fluid and blood.
* **Symptoms:** **sudden distortion (metamorphopsia),** a central blind spot (scotoma), and blurred central vision. Straight lines look wavy.
* **Diagnosis:** dilated exam, **OCT** (shows fluid), and fluorescein or OCT angiography.
* **Treatment:** **intravitreal anti-VEGF injections** (aflibercept, ranibizumab, bevacizumab, faricimab) given every few weeks, long-term. These can stabilize or improve vision.
* **Urgency:** any new distortion in a patient with drusen needs prompt ophthalmology review.

> **Clinical Pearl:** Patients with dry AMD should monitor their own vision daily with an Amsler grid. New distortion or a new central blank spot means it may have converted to wet AMD and needs same-week treatment.

## Other Macular Disorders

### Central Serous Chorioretinopathy
Fluid collects under the macula. Typically affects **young to middle-aged men** with stress, type A personality, or **corticosteroid use** (all routes). Presents with blurred or dim central vision, metamorphopsia, and a smaller image. Most resolve in months. Stop steroids if possible.

### Epiretinal Membrane
A thin scar-like membrane forms on the macular surface, causing distortion and blurring. Often idiopathic and age-related. Surgery (membrane peel) is considered if function is affected.

### Macular Hole
A full-thickness defect in the fovea. Causes central blur, distortion, and a central blind spot. Surgery (vitrectomy with gas bubble) can close it.

### Macular Edema
Fluid in the macula, from diabetes, vein occlusion, uveitis, or after cataract surgery. Treated by addressing the cause and using anti-VEGF, steroid, or anti-inflammatory therapy.

## Inherited Retinal Disease

### Retinitis Pigmentosa (RP)
A group of genetic diseases in which **rods** degenerate first, then cones.

* **Night blindness** is the first symptom, in adolescence
* Progressive **loss of peripheral vision** with **tunnel vision**
* **Bone spicule pigmentation** in the retina
* Can be associated with syndromes (**Usher syndrome**: deafness and RP)
* No cure, but supportive care and genetic counseling. Gene therapy exists for specific mutations.

### Stargardt Disease
An inherited macular dystrophy in young people, causing central vision loss.

## Intraocular Tumors (Brief)

* **Choroidal melanoma:** the most common primary intraocular cancer in adults. Often found incidentally as a dark elevated mass. Treated with radiation or enucleation. Liver is the most common site of metastasis.
* **Retinoblastoma:** children (Lesson 16).
* **Metastases:** the most common intraocular tumors overall, from breast and lung.

> **Summary**
> New flashes and floaters may signal a posterior vitreous detachment with a retinal tear and need a dilated exam. Retinal detachment presents as a painless curtain over vision. Macula-on cases are emergencies. Gas bubbles forbid air travel and nitrous oxide. AMD causes central vision loss: dry AMD has drusen and is treated with AREDS2 and smoking cessation, while wet AMD causes distortion and needs anti-VEGF injections.
