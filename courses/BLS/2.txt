# Scene Size-Up and Patient Assessment

**Scene Size-Up** refers to the first look at a new environment. This is more applicable to prehospital providers (EMTs and paramedics), because they need to ensure that the scene is safe, take safety actions (e.g., masking up, hazmat gear), and determine if more resources are required to address the situation. In the hospital context, you should always ensure that the environment remains safe. __Your safety__ comes before your colleagues', and your colleagues' before your patients'. While this may seem backward, the rationale is that a provider injured becomes another patient themselves. Therefore, you should seek to preserve your own safety at all times.
* This safety also includes wearing proper protective equipment. __Standard precautions__ refers to all actions that are taken to prevent disease spread. This typically includes __medical asepsis__, washed hands with clean technique, alongside gloves and a mask. As you are likely aware, other PPE equipment exist, including gowns, surgical caps, and face shields.

Once the scene has been determined to be safe, the process of **patient assessment** begins. Patient assessment is a broad term for uncovering diagnostic cues within a patient, in other words, identifying pertinent signs, symptoms, and other conditions of the patient. 
> A __pertinent negative__ is the absence of a symptom, physical finding, or lab result where it would be expected; e.g., chest pain in a heart attack.
There are multiple ways to assess the patient, and as a doctor, it is recommended to move away from static flows and investigate more independently. However, we will introduce the common patient assessment model used in Basic Life Support for this course.
## Primary Assessment
* Form a __general impression__: this includes sex, race, facial expression, and other characteristics of the patient. While difficult in Roblox, this represents the first impression of the patient. Do they look extremely ill? Breathing with their hands supported on their knees (Called __tripod breathing__, a sign of respiratory distress)? The first look at a patient can reveal their general status.
* Assess the level of consciousness: this can be accomplished using the AVPU or GCS scales. AVPU is the most general and easiest scale to quickly do. It stands for __a__lert, __v__ocal, __p__ain, or __u__nresponsive. It is typically document like "A/AVPU." Simply, you document if the patient is readily responsive. If they are already responsive, looking at you, and freely moving, then they're alert. If they require a question or vocal stimulus, they're vocal. If they elicit a response only after a small pinch (this is typically done in the trapezius above the clavicle), then they are pain; if no stimuli elicit any response then they are unresponsive.
> Typically, the next steps in the primary assessment are the classic ABCs: airway, breathing, and circulation. __If the patient is unresponsive__, the proper order is now **C**AB.
* Assess the airway: If the patient is talking and has no complaints of the airway, then there is likely no need to assess it (the primary survey looks only for *immediate*, life-threatening injuries. The assessment of the airway must be done by opening it, and there are two techniques to do so. For real-life skills, you may look up photos of the techniques, as they are simple but would be annoying to comprehend and describe in text. The first-line option is head-tilt chin-lift, as it has been shown to be more effective at opening the airway and freeing the tongue from the back of the throat (this is a significant, common problem if a patient goes unconscious, and is the entire reason why airway management is taught in BLS; choking on the tongue is a real threat). However, head-tilt chin-lift moves the cervical vertebrae (during the head tilt), and therefore __cannot be used if there is a concern for cervical spine injury__. This includes falls, drowning, motor accidents, etc. If the airway is not clear, suction or other tools may be available. If the patient is unconscious but has vomitus, roll them to their side into __recovery position__ (lateral decubitus).
* Assess the breathing: Auscultate the breathing, noting the rate of breathing. A notable sound may also be heard when auscultating, for instance, rales, crackles, wheezing, stridor, or more. Stridor typically indicates an upper airway obstruction, and wheezing a lower airway obstruction. Crackles is associated with fluid in the lungs (pulmonary edema). Treatment options will be explored in future courses.
* Assess the circulation: check pulse. This is done at one of three points, depending on age and other factors. For conscious adults and children, use the radial. For unconscious adults and children, use the carotid. For unconscious infants, use the brachial. Vital signs will be explored in future lessons. Another important consideration is the capillary refill time (CRT), which should be <2 sec. Simply press down on the fingernail bed and time how long color comes back into the nail. A higher time could indicate difficulties with blood circulation.
* Primary Assessment: look from head-to-toe if the patient is in a critical condition. In true emergencies, trauma shears are used to remove the patient's clothing in order to quickly view all injuries, but this is obviously not required for medical patients. Look carefully at any involved sites and document your findings as accurately as possible.
## Beyond the Primary Assessment
After the primary assessment, you will take a history, perform a secondary assessment and subsequent reassessments. For the purposes of this course, only the primary assessment needs to be emphasized.
